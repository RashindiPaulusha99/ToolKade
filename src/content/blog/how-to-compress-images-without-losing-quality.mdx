## Why images are bigger than they need to be

Most images are saved with far more detail than the place they are shown in can display:

- **Too many pixels.** A phone photo is often around 4,000 pixels wide, while a phone screen, a blog post or an email preview shows a fraction of that.
- **More quality than the eye can see.** Cameras save at very high quality settings so photos can be edited and printed.
- **The wrong format.** A photo saved as PNG can be many times larger than the same photo as JPG or WebP.

Fix those three things and most of the file size disappears.

## How to compress images without losing quality

### 1. Resize to the size you actually need

Resizing is the single biggest saving, because file size grows with the number of pixels, which grows with width *times* height.

For example, a typical <strong>{camera.width} × {camera.height}</strong> phone photo has <strong>{megapixels(camera)} megapixels</strong>. Scaled to <strong>{target.width}</strong> pixels wide, it becomes <strong>{target.width} × {target.height}</strong>, just <strong>{megapixels(target)} megapixels</strong>, about <strong>{share}%</strong> of the original pixel count, and still sharper than most screens need for a full-width image.

Good targets:

| Where the image goes | Width to aim for |
| --- | --- |
| Full-width website banner | 1,920 px |
| Blog post or article image | 1,200–1,600 px |
| Email or social media post | 1,080–1,600 px |
| Thumbnail or profile picture | 400–800 px |

### 2. Choose the right format

- **WebP**: usually the smallest file for the same visual quality. Supported by all modern browsers.
- **JPG**: the most compatible choice for photos, including older software.
- **PNG**: lossless, best for screenshots, logos and graphics with transparency or sharp text. Avoid it for photos.

### 3. Lower the quality a little

JPG and WebP let you choose a **quality** level. They save space by discarding fine detail that is hard to see. Between about 70% and 85%, most photos look the same as the original. Go lower only if you need a very small file, and check for blocky edges or smudged skin tones.

### 4. Compare before you delete

Look at the compressed image at the size people will see it. If it looks the same, you are done. Keep your original for printing or future editing.

## Common mistakes and tips

**Compressing the same JPG again and again.** Each lossy save discards a little more detail. Always compress from the original, not from an already compressed copy.

**Using PNG for photos.** PNG keeps every pixel exactly, which makes photos huge. Convert photos to WebP or JPG.

**Scaling images up.** Enlarging a small image makes the file bigger and the picture blurrier. Only scale down.

**Forgetting about privacy.** Photos can carry hidden data such as the GPS location where they were taken. Re-saving through a compressor that strips metadata removes it.

**Uploading private photos to unknown websites.** Many online compressors upload your files to their servers. A browser-based tool processes them on your own device instead.

## Conclusion

To compress images without losing quality, resize them to the size they will be shown at, pick the right format (WebP or JPG for photos, PNG for graphics) and use a quality setting around 70–85%. Together these usually shrink files dramatically with no visible difference.
